      The legal ground

      The United States Code

      The United States Code (U.S.C.) is the official compilation of the general and permanent laws Congress has passed, organized by subject into 54 titles. When federal courts decide cases, they apply these statutes and interpret what they mean.

      What it is

      Prepared by the Office of the Law Revision Counsel of the U.S. House of Representatives. Titles enacted as “positive law” are legal evidence of the law; the remaining titles are prima facie evidence of it.

      Source: uscode.house.gov →

      How courts use it

      The Constitution sits above the Code. Courts apply the Code's sections to the facts of each case, and their published decisions interpreting those sections become precedent that later courts follow.

      What it doesn't cover

      The Code does not contain court decisions, agency regulations or state laws. Most trust, probate and creditor law is state law — such as each state's version of the Uniform Trust Code — and is decided in state courts.

      Source: uscode.house.gov →

      Evaluation

      The whole diagram, compared with the law

      What the diagram claims overall, what U.S. law provides, and what trusts can and cannot do.

      The claim

      The diagram depicts a path from public institutions, through a “public nexus” and the National Reform Protocol, to private equity and fully protected assets.

      What the law says

      No statute or court decision recognizes that path. Liability, taxation and creditor rights are set by specific laws, and a person's declared status or a trust's label does not change them.

      What trusts can legitimately do

      A properly drafted and funded trust can avoid probate, provide for management if you become incapacitated, and — in some irrevocable forms — limit creditor access within the limits state law sets.

      What trusts cannot do

      A trust cannot eliminate taxes you owe, place assets beyond the reach of courts, or shield property transferred to avoid existing creditors. The IRS treats trusts used to evade taxes as abusive.
